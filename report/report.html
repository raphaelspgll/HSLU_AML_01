Introduction

With the rapid expansion of heat pump installations across Swiss households, electricity demand is increasingly driven by heating and cooling needs rather than fixed consumption habits. Unlike traditional resistive heating, heat pumps draw substantial power in short, weather-dependent bursts. This creates load profiles that are harder to predict and more sensitive to outdoor conditions than the appliance-driven consumption patterns that grid operators have historically managed.

For energy suppliers and grid operators, this shift has direct economic consequences. Procurement decisions made a day or week in advance are based on load forecasts. When a cold snap arrives earlier than expected or a warm spell pushes temperatures above the heating threshold, actual demand diverges from the submitted schedule. The resulting imbalances must be settled at spot or balancing energy prices. These costs ultimately flow back to households and erode the economic case for electrification if not properly managed.

Against this background, this analysis systematically evaluates smart meter data from a portfolio of heat pump households in Switzerland, using the HEAPO dataset, in order to identify the most important drivers of electricity consumption and build a well-founded basis for demand modelling. The focus is not only on explaining consumption variability across seasonal patterns, building characteristics, and technology choices, but also on quantifying the interaction between external factors such as outdoor temperature, heating degree days, and building type.

The report is structured as follows. Section 2 presents exploratory data analysis. Section 3 fits and interprets six models, namely a Linear Model, GLM Poisson, GLM Binomial, Generalised Additive Model, Neural Network, and Support Vector Machine. Section 4 compares model performance. Section 5 draws conclusions for energy suppliers and grid operators.

Dataset

Source: HEAPO – Household Energy and Smart Meter Data (Zenodo, 2025).

The HEAPO dataset was collected as part of a Swiss research project monitoring residential heat pump installations via smart meters. It covers 99838 household-days across 127 households observed between 2022 and 2024, paired with daily weather data from the corresponding measurement region. Each observation represents one household on one calendar day, with daily electricity consumption as the primary outcome alongside a set of building, technology, and weather covariates.

Exploratory Data Analysis

Daily electricity consumption (kWh_total) is right-skewed on the raw scale and approximately symmetric after log transformation. The log-transformed variable log_kWh_total is used as the continuous response in all regression models. The binary variable high_consumption is derived from it. A day is classified as high consumption if daily log_kWh_total exceeds that household’s own 75th percentile, flagging the top 25% of consumption days for each individual household.

Modelling

Six models are fitted to the HEAPO data, each targeting a response variable suited to its statistical family. The Linear Model and Generalised Additive Model are fitted to log_kWh_total, the log-transformed daily electricity consumption. The GLM Binomial, Neural Network, and Support Vector Machine all classify household-days as high or low consumption using each household’s own 75th percentile as the threshold, capturing the top 25% of days for each individual household. The GLM Poisson models the monthly count of high-consumption days per household, capturing longer-term demand patterns at a coarser aggregation. Model performance is evaluated in the comparison section: regression models are compared via five-fold cross-validation MAE in kWh and R²; classification models via AUC and accuracy.

Linear Model (LM)

Lead: Tharrmeehan Krishnathasan

Objective

This section models daily household energy consumption as \(\text{log\_kWh\_total}\), the natural logarithm of total daily kilowatt-hour usage. The log transformation is standard for energy data: daily kWh is strictly positive and right-skewed in raw form, and approximately normal on the log scale.

Predictors cover weather conditions (heating degree days, sunshine hours, humidity), building characteristics (building type, living area, heat pump type), and household composition (number of residents). Two interaction terms are added to test whether cold-weather sensitivity differs by building type and heat pump technology.

Model Setup

Two models are estimated:

Simple MLR: all seven predictors with main effects only, as an interpretable baseline for the additive relationships between predictors and log energy consumption:

\[ \log(\text{kWh}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{HDD}_i + \beta_2\,\text{Sunshine}_i + \cdots + \beta_7\,\text{HeatpumpType}_i + \varepsilon_i \]

Extended MLR: adds two interaction terms motivated by domain knowledge:

  • \(\text{heating\_degree\_days} \times \text{heatpump\_type}\): different heat pump technologies have different efficiencies in cold conditions, so the effect of cold on consumption should vary by pump type.
  • \(\text{heating\_degree\_days} \times \text{building\_type}\): houses and apartments differ structurally in size, exposed surface area, and construction, so their cold-weather sensitivity is expected to differ.

All coefficients are on the log scale: a coefficient of 0.05 means a 5% multiplicative change in kWh for a one-unit increase in the predictor.

The panel structure (multiple observations per household) means the i.i.d. assumption of standard OLS is not fully met; standard errors may be slightly understated due to within-household correlation.

Key Findings

Heating degree days is the dominant weather driver: Cold weather is the primary positive predictor of energy consumption. Houses start from a higher baseline (β = 0.220) and are more sensitive to cold than apartments (interaction β = 0.039, p < 0.001), consistent with lower thermal efficiency.

Household size has the strongest marginal effect: With β = 0.134, each additional resident increases daily consumption by ~14.3%, making it the largest single-predictor effect in the model after the building type interaction.

Ground-source heat pumps are more efficient and more stable: They consume ~13.3% less at baseline and respond less to temperature swings (interaction β = −0.003), consistent with the efficiency advantage of ground-source systems at low outdoor temperatures.

Humidity is not a useful predictor: humidity_avg is not significant (p = 0.165) once the other weather variables are controlled for.

Overall explanatory power is limited: The extended model explains ~27.4% of the variance in log daily consumption (adj. R² = 0.2743). On the original kWh scale, mean absolute error is 11 kWh/day (5-fold CV). Household-level heterogeneity is the likely cause; models with household-level random effects would be needed for operational forecasting.

GLM – Poisson

Lead: Raphaël Spagolla

Objective

The aim of this analysis is to model the number of high-consumption days per household-month (\(n_{high\ days}\)). This variable represents count data, taking non-negative integer values. A plain linear model is not appropriate here, as it assumes normally distributed errors and can produce negative predictions — both of which are incompatible with count outcomes.

A Poisson Generalised Linear Model (GLM) is used instead, as it is specifically designed for non-negative integer outcomes and naturally allows the variance to grow with the mean. By including an offset for the number of observed days, the model estimates rates, allowing interpretation of results in terms of relative changes (rate ratios).

Model Setup

n_high_days ~ heating_degree_days + temp_avg + living_area + 
    building_type + heatpump_type + has_floor_heating + n_residents + 
    share_weekend

The response variable is the number of high-consumption days per household-month (\(n_{high\ days}\)). A day is classified as a high-consumption day if its daily log_kWh_total exceeds that household’s own 75th percentile, consistent with the threshold used in the GLM Binomial and throughout the report. Because the number of observed days can vary slightly across household–months, the model includes the logarithm of the observed days \((\log(n_{days}))\) as an offset. This adjustment effectively converts the outcome into a rate — high-consumption days per observed day — so that observations remain comparable even when exposure differs between months.

To model these rates, a Poisson GLM with a log-link is fitted:

\[ \log(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{pi} + \log(n\_days_i) \]

The log-link guarantees positive fitted values and supports a multiplicative interpretation of effects.

One predictor warrants clarification: share_weekend is the monthly proportion of weekend days within a household–month (a continuous value between 0 and 1), and is distinct from the daily binary is_weekend flag used in the GLM Binomial and other daily-level models.

Model Results

The results are interpreted in terms of percentage changes in the expected rate, holding all other variables constant.

Building type (house vs apartments): Households living in houses have a rate of high-consumption days approximately 363% higher than those living in apartments \(((4.632 - 1) \times 100\%)\). This is by far the strongest effect in the model, clearly visible in the boxplot, and indicates that building type is the dominant structural driver of consumption patterns.

Average temperature: The rate ratio of 0.915 implies that each additional degree Celsius is associated with an 8.5% decrease in the expected rate of high- consumption days \(((1 - 0.915) \times 100\%)\). This reflects reduced heating needs at warmer temperatures, consistent with the strong negative trend visible in the scatterplot.

Heating degree days: The rate ratio is marginally below 1 (\(\approx\) 0.999) and not statistically significant. Once average temperature is already in the model, heating degree days add little additional explanatory power, as both variables capture overlapping thermal information. This collinearity explains why the coefficient is effectively zero.

Number of residents: Each additional household member increases the expected rate by approximately 8.9% \(((1.089 - 1) \times 100\%)\). This reflects the intuitive result that larger households consume more energy overall.

Living area: The rate ratio of 1.001 indicates a statistically significant but practically small positive effect — each additional square metre increases the expected rate by about 0.1%. The effect accumulates for larger dwellings but remains modest compared to building type or household size.

Ground-source heat pump: Households equipped with a ground-source heat pump show a rate approximately 9.3% lower than the reference heat pump type \(((1 - 0.907) \times 100\%)\). This is consistent with ground-source systems being more energy-efficient, particularly during colder periods.

Floor heating: Households with floor heating show a rate approximately 13% higher than those without \(((1.130 - 1) \times 100\%)\). This likely reflects the greater energy input required by radiant floor systems to maintain comfort temperatures.

Weekend share: This predictor has a very wide confidence interval and is non-significant once overdispersion is accounted for (quasi-Poisson p > 0.05). The direction of the raw Poisson estimate should not be interpreted. The two models are not directly comparable on this predictor, as they operate at different aggregation levels.

Overall, structural and household characteristics (building type, size, and number of residents) exhibit the strongest and most reliable effects, while temperature captures an important environmental influence.

Model Diagnostics

A key assumption of the Poisson GLM is equidispersion, i.e., that the conditional variance equals the conditional mean. We assess this by comparing the residual deviance to the residual degrees of freedom. For the fitted Poisson model, the residual deviance is 27,529.6 on 3291 df, yielding a ratio of 8.37. Since this substantially exceeds 1, there is strong evidence of overdispersion — the Poisson model underestimates standard errors and overstates statistical significance.

We therefore refit the model using a quasi-Poisson family, which retains identical point estimates but inflates standard errors by \(\sqrt{\hat{\phi}}\) (\(\hat{\phi}\) = 7.74), yielding more conservative and reliable inference. The most visible consequence is that weekend share shifts from significant under Poisson (p = 0.028) to clearly non-significant under quasi-Poisson (p = 0.43), indicating that this effect should not be over-interpreted.

The table and plot below show the SE inflation across all predictors. The effect is proportional — predictors with larger SEs to begin with (e.g., weekend share, house vs apartments) show the largest absolute increase, while those with very small SEs (e.g., living area, number of residents) are inflated by a smaller absolute amount but the same factor \(\sqrt{\hat{\phi}}\).

Key Findings

The Poisson GLM provides a clear and interpretable account of the factors driving the frequency of high-consumption days. Building type, temperature, and household size emerge as the strongest and most robust predictors, consistent with both domain knowledge and the exploratory analysis.

However, a dispersion ratio of 8.37 confirms substantial overdispersion, making standard Poisson inference unreliable. The quasi-Poisson model corrects for this by inflating standard errors appropriately, leading to more conservative and trustworthy conclusions — most notably, the loss of significance for weekend share.

While the quasi-Poisson model improves inference, a negative binomial model could be considered as a more flexible alternative, as it models the overdispersion through an explicit distributional parameter rather than a post- hoc correction.

GLM – Binomial

Lead: Emanuel Lemma

Objective

Can we predict whether a given day will be unusually high-consumption for a specific household? This section fits a Generalised Linear Model with a binomial family to answer exactly that question. The response variable high_consumption is a binary indicator that equals 1 if the household’s daily log_kWh_total exceeds that household’s own 75th percentile and 0 otherwise. Using a per-household threshold means the model is not distinguishing houses from apartments. Each household’s own consumption baseline is already accounted for. Instead, the model learns what drives a day to be in the top 25% for a given household, regardless of its size or type. By modelling the log-odds of high consumption as a linear function of weather conditions, building characteristics, and calendar effects, we obtain a model that is both interpretable and actionable for a client interested in understanding what drives demand spikes at the household level.

For each of the 127 households, the 75th percentile of their log_kWh_total is computed and used as that household’s personal high-consumption threshold. A day is labelled 1 if it falls in the top 25% of that household’s own consumption distribution. By construction, every household contributes exactly 25% high-consumption days, so building type and household size are absorbed into the threshold and do not mechanically drive the outcome. The dataset is restricted to nine predictors covering weather conditions, building characteristics, and calendar effects, then split into 80% training and 20% test sets. The model is fitted with glm() using family = binomial, which models the log-odds of high consumption as a linear combination of predictors. The saved model is reloaded on subsequent knits to avoid refitting.

A striking result of using the per-household threshold is that structural predictors (building type, heat pump type, living area, and number of residents) are all statistically non-significant. This is expected because the threshold is set individually for each household, and these characteristics are already accounted for in the baseline. The model is therefore answering a different and more useful question. Given how this household normally behaves, what makes a specific day unusually high?

The coefficient table reveals several key patterns.

  • Weekend: The dominant predictor, with OR = 1.57. Days falling on a Saturday or Sunday have 57% higher odds of being a top-25% consumption day for that household. Households spend more time at home on weekends, increasing both heating and appliance usage regardless of building type or size.
  • Average temperature: Each additional degree Celsius reduces the odds by 17.7% (OR = 0.823, p < 0.001). Colder days push households above their own typical usage level through increased heat pump operation.
  • Heating degree days: Each additional HDD increases the odds by 6.4% (OR = 1.064, p < 0.001). HDD and temperature are collinear and together capture the thermal load on the heat pump. Both are retained because HDD captures heating demand only and is zero on warm days, while temp_avg can detect cooling demand from reversible heat pumps, a pattern HDD alone would miss entirely.
  • Building type, heat pump type, living area, number of residents, floor heating: None of these are statistically significant (all p > 0.05). Once the model conditions on a household’s own 75th percentile, these structural characteristics carry no additional explanatory power for day-to-day variation in consumption.
  • Month: Spring and autumn show the strongest reductions relative to January. April and October have the lowest odds (OR 0.29 and 0.28 respectively), as these are transition months when neither heating nor cooling is needed. Notably, June and July are not significantly different from January (p = 0.31 and 0.09), suggesting that some households also produce high-consumption days in summer, likely from heat pump cooling or increased hot water use. December is likewise indistinguishable from January (p = 0.41). The full seasonal pattern is visible in the odds ratio plot below.

Generalised Additive Model (GAM)

Lead: Emanuel Lemma

Objective

The Linear Model assumes that each predictor affects the response in a straight line. For energy consumption, this is often too rigid. The relationship between heating demand and electricity use may differ between houses and apartments, and between heat pump types, in ways that cannot be captured by fixed linear slopes. A Generalised Additive Model (GAM) relaxes this assumption by replacing fixed slopes with flexible smooth functions, while remaining fully interpretable through its component plots. Here we model log_kWh_total using exactly the same predictors as the extended Linear Model, namely heating degree days (interacted with building type and heat pump type), sunshine hours, humidity, living area, and the number of residents. The GAM replaces the LM’s explicit interaction terms with by = smooths, which allow the shape of the heating-demand curve to vary freely between groups. This makes the comparison between the two models direct and fair.

The model uses by = smooths for heating degree days, which allow the heating- demand curve to take a completely different shape for each building type and heat pump type, equivalent to the interaction terms in the extended LM but without constraining the relationship to be linear. All smooths are penalised regression splines with automatic smoothness selection via restricted maximum likelihood.

\[ \begin{align} \log(\text{kWh}_i) = \; & \alpha + \beta_1 \text{building type}_i + \beta_2 \text{heatpump type}_i + \beta_3 \text{n residents}_i \\ & + s(\text{HDD}_i \mid \text{building type}_i) + s(\text{HDD}_i \mid \text{heatpump type}_i) \\ & + s(\text{sunshine hours}_i) + s(\text{humidity avg}_i) + s(\text{living area}_i) + \epsilon_i \end{align} \]

where \(s(\cdot)\) denotes penalised regression splines with automatic smoothness selection via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML), which balances fit and smoothness automatically, and the \(by =\) smooths allow the shape of the heating-demand curve to vary freely between groups rather than constraining it to a fixed linear slope.

Neural Network (NN)

Lead: Raphaël Spagolla

Objective

The aim of this analysis is to predict whether an observation corresponds to a high-consumption event (high_consumption), a binary outcome taking values 0 or 1. This is a classification problem, where the focus is on accurately distinguishing between high- and low-consumption cases.

From a practical perspective, reliable classification of high-consumption events can support decision-making processes such as resource allocation, cost management, or demand planning. Early identification of such events allows for more efficient operational responses.

A neural network is employed to capture potentially complex and non-linear relationships between consumption and predictors, including weather conditions, building characteristics, and household composition. Model performance is evaluated based on predictive accuracy and classification metrics derived from test data.

Data Preparation

The dataset consists of 99,838 observations and includes variables related to weather conditions, building characteristics, and household composition. The response variable, high_consumption, is binary, defined using each household’s own 75th percentile of log_kWh_total as the threshold — consistent with the GLM Binomial and SVM. By construction, exactly 25% of each household’s days are classified as high-consumption.

To ensure valid model evaluation, the data is split into training (80%) and test (20%) sets using a stratified sampling approach. This preserves the class distribution in both subsets and avoids biased performance estimates that could arise from imbalanced splits.

Categorical variables are transformed into dummy variables using one-hot encoding, allowing them to be used as inputs in the neural network. In addition, all predictors are scaled based on the training data. This step is important for neural networks, as it improves numerical stability and facilitates efficient training. The same transformations are then applied to the test set to ensure consistency and prevent data leakage.

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Lead: Tharrmeehan Krishnathasan

Objective

This section classifies each household-day as high consumption (top 25% of that household’s days) or normal, a binary problem with practical relevance: knowing which days are likely to be high-load in advance lets grid operators and energy providers plan capacity.

An SVM is used because it can find non-linear decision boundaries via kernel functions without requiring distributional assumptions about the predictors. Performance is evaluated on a held-out test set using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score.

Data Preparation

high_consumption labels a day as high if it falls in the top 25% of that household’s own log_kWh_total distribution. For each of the 127 households, the 75th percentile is computed separately, so every household contributes exactly 25% high-consumption days by construction.

After removing missing values, the dataset has 93,686 observations: 23,440 high-consumption days (25.0%) and 70,246 normal days (75.0%). log_kWh_total is excluded to prevent data leakage; the SVM must identify high-load days from weather and household characteristics alone.

The data are split 80/20 into training (74,949 rows) and test (18,737 rows) using stratified sampling to keep the class ratio consistent. All numeric predictors are standardised before training, as SVMs are sensitive to feature scale.

Model Setup

Both models use a radial basis function (RBF) kernel, which maps data into a higher-dimensional space to find non-linear decision boundaries. This suits energy data where weather and household effects interact in ways a linear boundary cannot capture.

Baseline SVM: C = 1, sigma estimated from training data. This provides a reference for the tuned model.

Tuned SVM: 5-fold cross-validation over a grid of C ∈ {1, 10, 100} and sigma ∈ {0.01, 0.1, 1/7}. C controls the misclassification penalty (higher = tighter fit); sigma is the inverse bandwidth of the RBF kernel (higher = more local boundaries). The combination with the highest CV accuracy is selected.

The best parameters were C = 100, sigma = 0.1429 (= 1/7), achieving CV accuracy of 0.8304 (Kappa = 0.4859) across the 3×3 grid.

Key Findings

The SVM achieves reasonable accuracy but low sensitivity: At 85.3% accuracy, the model scores roughly 8 percentage points above the naive majority-class baseline (74.9%). Sensitivity of 59% is the bigger concern: more than half of actual high-consumption days are missed, while specificity of 94.1% shows the model rarely raises false alarms on normal days. This makes it too conservative on its own for capacity planning or demand-response triggering.

Tuning yields real but limited improvement: Moving from default parameters to C=100, sigma=0.1429 improves sensitivity from 58.1% to 59% and accuracy from 85.1% to 85.3%. The gains are consistent but small; further tuning will not resolve the core problem.

The predictor set is the bottleneck: Weather conditions and static household characteristics capture only part of what drives high-consumption days. High and normal consumption days overlap substantially in the feature space, and no kernel or regularisation choice can separate what is genuinely mixed in the data. Richer features would help: lagged consumption, occupancy patterns, or appliance-level data.

Threshold adjustment is a practical alternative: Lowering the flagging threshold would recover more true positives at the cost of more false alarms, which may be acceptable in a probabilistic forecasting pipeline. At the default threshold, the model should not be the sole basis for high-consumption day decisions.

Conclusions

Across 127 Swiss households monitored daily from 2022 to 2024, this analysis identified the key drivers of heat pump electricity consumption and built a foundation for demand modelling relevant to grid operators and energy suppliers.

Cold weather is the strongest structural driver of daily consumption, but its effect is not uniform. Houses show a sharply accelerating response on the coldest winter days, a pattern that has direct implications for load forecasting during cold snaps and that a plain linear model understates. Beyond weather, heat pump type and occupancy are the two most consistent predictors across all models. Ground-source heat pumps consume approximately 13% less electricity per day than air-source systems, and every additional resident adds roughly 14% to daily demand regardless of building type or season.

For classifying unusually high-demand days, weekend occupancy is the single strongest predictor, with cold weather second. Structural characteristics become non-significant once the model accounts for each household’s own baseline, meaning demand spikes are driven by behaviour and weather rather than building or system properties.

The GAM achieved the best predictive accuracy for daily consumption with a MAE of 10.6 kWh per day and an R² of 31%, modestly improving on the Linear Model. Both models leave roughly two thirds of day-to-day variation unexplained, pointing to the value of incorporating behavioural signals in future work. For classification, the Neural Network achieved the strongest performance with an AUC of 0.908 and accuracy of 85.5%, with the SVM close behind at an AUC of 0.842 and accuracy of 83.1%. For a client prioritising transparency, the GLM Binomial is recommended, achieving an AUC of 0.83 and accuracy of 80.2% while remaining fully interpretable.

The practical implication for energy suppliers and grid operators is to target demand management programmes at larger households with air-source systems during cold weather peaks, where high occupancy and less efficient heating combine to produce the sharpest load spikes. Weekend time-of-use tariffs or demand response incentives offer an additional lever for flattening the load peaks that drive balancing energy costs.
