\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

With the rapid expansion of heat pump installations across Swiss
households, electricity demand is increasingly driven by heating and
cooling needs rather than fixed consumption habits. Unlike traditional
resistive heating, heat pumps draw substantial power in short,
weather-dependent bursts. This creates load profiles that are harder to
predict and more sensitive to outdoor conditions than the
appliance-driven consumption patterns that grid operators have
historically managed.

For energy suppliers and grid operators, this shift has direct economic
consequences. Procurement decisions made a day or week in advance are
based on load forecasts. When a cold snap arrives earlier than expected
or a warm spell pushes temperatures above the heating threshold, actual
demand diverges from the submitted schedule. The resulting imbalances
must be settled at spot or balancing energy prices. These costs
ultimately flow back to households and erode the economic case for
electrification if not properly managed.

Against this background, this analysis systematically evaluates smart
meter data from a portfolio of heat pump households in Switzerland,
using the \textbf{HEAPO dataset}, in order to identify the most
important drivers of electricity consumption and build a well-founded
basis for demand modelling. The focus is not only on explaining
consumption variability across seasonal patterns, building
characteristics, and technology choices, but also on quantifying the
interaction between external factors such as outdoor temperature,
heating degree days, and building type.

\subsection{Dataset}\label{dataset}

\textbf{Source:} HEAPO -- Household Energy and Smart Meter Data (Zenodo,
2025).

The HEAPO dataset was collected as part of a Swiss research project
monitoring residential heat pump installations via smart meters. It
covers 99838 household-days across 127 households observed between 2022
and 2024, paired with daily weather data from the corresponding
measurement region. Each observation represents one household on one
calendar day, with daily electricity consumption as the primary outcome
alongside a rich set of building, technology, and weather covariates.

The raw HEAPO dataset spans 2018 to 2024 across 438 monitored households
in the canton of Zurich. The modelling subset was restricted to the
period 2022-01-01 through 2024-02-29 and limited to households with at
least 95\% daily coverage to exclude partially observed installations.
It was then randomly sampled to a maximum of 100,000 observations to
comply with the course data size limit. The final dataset contains 99838
household-days across 127 households.

\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}\label{exploratory-data-analysis}

Daily electricity consumption (\texttt{kWh\_total}) is right-skewed on
the raw scale and approximately symmetric after log transformation. The
log-transformed variable \texttt{log\_kWh\_total} is used as the
continuous response in all regression models. The binary variable
\texttt{high\_consumption} is derived from it: a day is classified as
high consumption if daily usage exceeds the household-level median.

Houses consume substantially more electricity per day than flats, with

controlling for weather, as the modelling section will confirm. Building

Taken together, the three plots tell a consistent story. Electricity
demand is primarily a seasonal phenomenon: the portfolio shifts from a
low-consumption summer baseline driven by domestic appliances to a
high-consumption winter regime dominated by heat pump space heating.
Within that seasonal pattern, building type is the strongest structural
separator, with houses drawing roughly twice the daily energy of flats
at any given temperature. These findings directly shape the modelling
choices below: the response is log-transformed to stabilise variance,
heating degree days enter as the primary continuous weather predictor,
and building type is retained as a categorical covariate in every model.
For the client, the key implication is that cold spells and the building
stock composition of a supply portfolio are the two dominant levers of
aggregate demand, and any forecasting tool must capture both to be
useful.

\section{Modelling}\label{modelling}

Six models are fitted to the HEAPO data, each targeting a response
variable suited to its statistical family. The Linear Model and
Generalised Additive Model are fitted to \texttt{log\_kWh\_total}, the
log-transformed daily electricity consumption. The GLM Binomial
classifies household-days as high or low consumption relative to each
household's own median. The Support Vector Machine classifies days as
high consumption using the global 75th percentile of daily kWh as the
threshold (top 25\% of usage). The Neural Network uses each household's
own median as its high-consumption threshold. The GLM Poisson models the
monthly count of high-consumption days per household, capturing
longer-term demand patterns at a coarser aggregation. Model performance
is evaluated in the comparison section: regression models are compared
via five-fold cross-validation MAE in kWh and R²; classification models
via AUC and accuracy.

\subsection{Linear Model (LM)}\label{lm}

\emph{Lead: Tharrmeehan Krishnathasan}

\subsubsection{Objective}\label{objective}

The aim of this analysis is to model daily household energy consumption
(\(\text{log\_kWh\_total}\)), the natural logarithm of total daily
kilowatt-hour usage. Log-transforming energy consumption is standard
practice: it is strictly positive, right-skewed on the original scale,
and approximately normal after transformation, making linear regression
appropriate.

Predictors include weather conditions (heating degree days, sunshine
hours, humidity), building characteristics (building type, living area,
heat pump type), and household composition (number of residents). By
including interaction terms, the model also captures how the sensitivity
to cold weather differs across building and heat pump types.

\subsubsection{Model Setup}\label{model-setup}

Two models are estimated:

\textbf{Simple MLR} --- all seven predictors with main effects only.
This serves as an interpretable baseline capturing the additive
relationships between predictors and log energy consumption:

\[
\log(\text{kWh}_i) = \beta_0 + \beta_1\,\text{HDD}_i + \beta_2\,\text{Sunshine}_i + \cdots + \beta_7\,\text{HeatpumpType}_i + \varepsilon_i
\]

\textbf{Extended MLR} --- adds two domain-motivated interaction terms:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\text{heating\_degree\_days} \times \text{heatpump\_type}\):
  different heat pump technologies have different efficiencies in cold
  conditions, so the effect of cold on consumption should vary by pump
  type.
\item
  \(\text{heating\_degree\_days} \times \text{building\_type}\):
  well-insulated houses are less sensitive to outdoor temperature than
  poorly-insulated ones.
\end{itemize}

All coefficients are on the log scale: a coefficient of 0.05 means a 5\%
multiplicative change in kWh for a one-unit increase in the predictor.

Note that the panel structure of the data (multiple daily observations
per household) means the i.i.d. assumption of standard OLS is not fully
met --- standard errors may be slightly understated due to
within-household correlation.

All predictors are statistically significant at the 0.001 level, with
the exception of \texttt{humidity\_avg} (p = 0.165), which does not
contribute meaningfully once the other variables are included. Key
effects on log energy consumption, holding all other variables constant:

\textbf{Heating degree days} (β = 0.021, p \textless{} 0.001): Each
additional HDD increases log consumption by \textasciitilde2.1\% for
air-source pump households in flats --- the baseline group. This is the
reference effect before interaction adjustments.

\textbf{Sunshine hours} (β = −0.015, p \textless{} 0.001): An additional
sunshine hour reduces consumption by \textasciitilde1.5\%, reflecting
lower heating demand on brighter days.

\textbf{Number of residents} (β = 0.134, p \textless{} 0.001): Each
additional resident increases consumption by \textasciitilde14.3\%
(\(e^{0.134} - 1\)), the second strongest effect after the
temperature-building interaction.

\textbf{Building type --- house} (β = 0.220, p \textless{} 0.001):
Houses consume \textasciitilde24.6\% more than flats at the baseline
temperature, even before accounting for their greater sensitivity to
cold (see interaction below).

\textbf{Heat pump type --- ground-source} (β = −0.143, p \textless{}
0.001): Ground-source pump households consume \textasciitilde13.3\% less
than air-source households at the baseline temperature.

\textbf{Interaction: HDD × house} (β = 0.039, p \textless{} 0.001):
Houses are substantially \emph{more} sensitive to cold than flats ---
each additional HDD increases log consumption by an additional 3.9\% for
house households. This is the largest interaction effect and confirms
that houses are less thermally efficient.

\textbf{Interaction: HDD × ground-source} (β = −0.003, p \textless{}

\subsubsection{Key Findings}\label{key-findings}

\textbf{Heating degree days is the dominant weather driver:} Cold
weather is the primary positive predictor of energy consumption, with an
effect that is further amplified in houses (interaction β = 0.039, p
\textless{} 0.001) --- confirming that houses are thermally less
efficient than flats.

\textbf{Household size has the strongest marginal effect:} With β =
0.134, each additional resident increases daily consumption by
\textasciitilde14.3\%, making it the largest single-predictor effect in
the model after the building type interaction.

\textbf{Houses consume substantially more, especially in winter:} Both
the baseline offset (β = 0.220, house vs.~flat) and the temperature
interaction (β = 0.039) point in the same direction --- houses face
higher base consumption and are more sensitive to cold.

\textbf{Ground-source heat pumps are more efficient and more stable:}
They consume \textasciitilde13.3\% less at baseline and respond less to
temperature swings (interaction β = −0.003), consistent with the known
efficiency advantage of ground-source systems.

\textbf{Humidity is not a useful predictor:} After controlling for other
variables, \texttt{humidity\_avg} is not statistically significant (p =
0.165) and contributes negligible explanatory power.

\textbf{Overall explanatory power is limited:} The extended model
explains only \textasciitilde27.4\% of the variance in log daily
consumption (adj. R² = 0.2743). On the original kWh scale the mean
absolute error is approximately 11 kWh/day (5-fold CV), meaning a
typical prediction deviates by that amount from the observed daily
consumption.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

The linear model successfully identifies the key structural and
weather-related drivers of daily household energy consumption and
provides clear, interpretable coefficient estimates. All predictors
except humidity are statistically significant, and the interaction terms
meaningfully capture the heterogeneous effect of cold weather across
building and pump types.

However, the model is of \textbf{limited usefulness for prediction}. An
adjusted R² of 0.27 means the model leaves \textasciitilde73\% of the
variance in daily consumption unexplained. At the household-day level,
predictions are too imprecise to serve as a reliable forecasting tool.
The likely cause is the strong household-level heterogeneity that a
cross-sectional linear model cannot capture --- different households
have very different baseline consumption levels regardless of the
measured predictors.

The model is better suited for \textbf{inference and understanding
drivers} than for prediction. It provides a statistically well-grounded
description of which factors are associated with higher consumption and
by how much, which is valuable for policy and planning contexts. For
operational forecasting, more flexible models with household-level
effects (e.g., mixed models, GAMs, or tree- based methods) would be
needed.

\subsection{GLM -- Poisson}\label{glm-poisson}

\emph{Lead: Raphaël Spagolla}

\subsubsection{Objective}\label{objective-1}

The aim of this analysis is to model the number of high-consumption days
per household-month (\(n_{high\ days}\)). This variable represents count
data, taking non-negative integer values. A plain linear model is not
appropriate here, as it assumes normally distributed errors and can
produce negative predictions --- both of which are incompatible with
count outcomes.

A Poisson Generalised Linear Model (GLM) is used instead, as it is
specifically designed for non-negative integer outcomes and naturally
allows the variance to grow with the mean. By including an offset for
the number of observed days, the model estimates rates, allowing
interpretation of results in terms of relative changes (rate ratios).

\subsubsection{Model Setup}\label{model-setup-1}

\begin{verbatim}
## n_high_days ~ heating_degree_days + temp_avg + living_area +      building_type + heatpump_type + has_floor_heating + n_residents +      share_weekend
\end{verbatim}

\begin{verbatim}
## glm(formula = form, family = poisson(link = "log"), data = dat_count, 
##     offset = log_n_days)
\end{verbatim}

The response variable is the number of high-consumption days per
household-month (\(n_{high\ days}\)). Because the number of observed
days can vary slightly across household--months, the model includes the
logarithm of the observed days \((\log(n_{days}))\) as an offset. This
adjustment effectively converts the outcome into a rate ---
high-consumption days per observed day --- so that observations remain
comparable even when exposure differs between months.

To model these rates, a Poisson GLM with a log-link is fitted:

\[
\log(\mathbb{E}[Y_i]) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X_{1i} + \cdots + \beta_p X_{pi} + \log(n\_days_i)
\]

The log-link guarantees positive fitted values and supports a
multiplicative interpretation of effects.

\subsubsection{Model Results}\label{model-results-1}

The results are interpreted in terms of \emph{percentage changes in the
expected rate}, holding all other variables constant.

\textbf{Building type (house vs flat):} Households living in houses have
a rate of high-consumption days approximately 363\% higher than those
living in flats \(((4.632 - 1) \times 100\%)\). This is by far the
strongest effect in the model, clearly visible in the boxplot, and
indicates that building type is the dominant structural driver of
consumption patterns.

\textbf{Average temperature:} The rate ratio of 0.915 implies that each
additional degree Celsius is associated with an 8.5\% decrease in the
expected rate of high- consumption days \(((1 - 0.915) \times 100\%)\).
This reflects reduced heating needs at warmer temperatures, consistent
with the strong negative trend visible in the scatterplot.

\textbf{Heating degree days:} The rate ratio is marginally below 1
(\(\approx\) 0.999) and not statistically significant. Once average
temperature is already in the model, heating degree days add little
additional explanatory power, as both variables capture overlapping
thermal information. This collinearity explains why the coefficient is
effectively zero.

\textbf{Number of residents:} Each additional household member increases
the expected rate by approximately 8.9\% \(((1.089 - 1) \times 100\%)\).
This reflects the intuitive result that larger households consume more
energy overall.

\textbf{Living area:} The rate ratio of 1.001 indicates a statistically
significant but practically small positive effect --- each additional
square metre increases the expected rate by about 0.1\%. The effect
accumulates for larger dwellings but remains modest compared to building
type or household size.

\textbf{Ground-source heat pump:} Households equipped with a
ground-source heat pump show a rate approximately 9.3\% lower than the
reference heat pump type \(((1 -
0.907) \times 100\%)\). This is consistent with ground-source systems
being more energy-efficient, particularly during colder periods.

\textbf{Floor heating:} Households with floor heating show a rate
approximately 13\% higher than those without
\(((1.130 - 1) \times 100\%)\). This likely reflects the greater energy
input required by radiant floor systems to maintain comfort
temperatures.

\textbf{Weekend share:} A higher proportion of weekend days within a
month is associated with a substantially lower rate of high-consumption
days (RR \(\approx\) 0.665). However, this effect has a very wide
confidence interval and becomes non-significant once overdispersion is
accounted for, suggesting considerable uncertainty in this estimate.

Overall, structural and household characteristics (building type, size,
and number of residents) exhibit the strongest and most reliable
effects, while temperature captures an important environmental
influence.

\subsubsection{Visual Evidence}\label{visual-evidence-1}

To complement the model output, the plots below provide direct visual
support for the main relationships in the data.

The \emph{boxplot by building type} shows a striking separation between
flats and houses. Flats cluster tightly with a median close to 3
high-consumption days and only two mild outliers, while houses display a
median around 16 days with a very wide interquartile range extending
from nearly 0 to 28. This large difference in both level and spread
confirms that building type is the dominant predictor in the model.

The \emph{scatterplot of high-consumption days versus average
temperature} reveals a strong and consistent negative pattern. At
temperatures near 0°C, many households record close to 30
high-consumption days, whereas above 15°C the counts concentrate near
zero. The smooth LOESS curve decreases steeply in the 0--10°C range and
flattens at higher temperatures, suggesting diminishing marginal effects
of warmth once heating demand is already low.

\subsubsection{Model Diagnostics}\label{model-diagnostics-1}

A key assumption of the Poisson GLM is \emph{equidispersion}, i.e., that
the conditional variance equals the conditional mean. We assess this by
comparing the residual deviance to the residual degrees of freedom. For
the fitted Poisson model, the residual deviance is
\ensuremath{2.75296\times 10^{4}} on 3291 df, yielding a ratio of 8.37.
Since this substantially exceeds 1, there is strong evidence of
\emph{overdispersion} --- the Poisson model underestimates standard
errors and overstates statistical significance.

We therefore refit the model using a \emph{quasi-Poisson} family, which
retains identical point estimates but inflates standard errors by
\(\sqrt{\hat{\phi}}\) (\(\hat{\phi}\) = 7.74), yielding more
conservative and reliable inference. The most visible consequence is
that \emph{weekend share} shifts from significant under Poisson (p =
0.028) to clearly non-significant under quasi-Poisson (p = 0.43),
indicating that this effect should not be over-interpreted.

The table and plot below show the SE inflation across all predictors.
The effect is proportional --- predictors with larger SEs to begin with
(e.g., \emph{weekend share}, \emph{house vs flat}) show the largest
absolute increase, while those with very small SEs (e.g., \emph{living
area}, \emph{number of residents}) are inflated by a smaller absolute
amount but the same factor \(\sqrt{\hat{\phi}}\).

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-1}

The Poisson GLM provides a clear and interpretable account of the
factors driving the frequency of high-consumption days. Building type,
temperature, and household size emerge as the strongest and most robust
predictors, consistent with both domain knowledge and the exploratory
analysis.

However, a dispersion ratio of 8.37 confirms substantial overdispersion,
making standard Poisson inference unreliable. The quasi-Poisson model
corrects for this by inflating standard errors appropriately, leading to
more conservative and trustworthy conclusions --- most notably, the loss
of significance for weekend share.

While the quasi-Poisson model improves inference, a negative binomial
model could be considered as a more flexible alternative, as it models
the overdispersion through an explicit distributional parameter rather
than a post- hoc correction.

\item
  \textbf{Floor heating:} OR = NA, a modest positive association likely
  driven by confounding with building size, as floor heating is more
  common in larger houses.
\item

  the odds of high consumption by 1.0586. The per-unit effect is small

methods in the following sections.

\subsection{Generalised Additive Model (GAM)}\label{gam}

\emph{Lead: Emanuel Lemma}

\subsubsection{Objective}\label{objective-3}

The Linear Model assumes that each predictor affects the response in a
straight line. For energy consumption, this is often too rigid. The
relationship between outdoor temperature and electricity use may
accelerate sharply below a certain cold threshold, and living area may
show diminishing returns at very large sizes. A \textbf{Generalised
Additive Model (GAM)} relaxes this assumption by replacing fixed slopes
with flexible smooth functions, while remaining fully interpretable
through its component plots. Here we model \texttt{log\_kWh\_total}, the
daily total electricity consumption on a log scale, using the same key
predictors as the Linear Model. This allows a direct comparison of
predictive performance and a visual check on whether non-linearity is
actually present in the data.

\item

  This is a modest but statistically reliable difference that likely
  reflects greater floor area rather than less efficient behaviour.
\item

  ground-source heat pump consume \textbf{12.5\% less} electricity per

  temperature year-round, making them significantly more efficient than
  air-source systems. Air-source systems struggle in very cold weather
  and must work harder to maintain indoor temperatures.
\end{itemize}

The smooth terms reveal where non-linearity is present in the continuous
predictors:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{s(heating\_degree\_days), edf = 1.0:} The smooth collapsed to
  a perfect straight line. Heating demand has a strictly linear effect
  on log(kWh), meaning every additional degree-day adds the same
  proportional increase in consumption. No threshold effects or
  accelerations are present.
\item
  \textbf{s(temp\_avg), edf = 7.0:} Highly non-linear. Temperature has a
  complex, season-dependent effect on consumption that cannot be
  captured with a single slope. This likely reflects the interplay
  between outdoor temperature, heating demand, and household behaviour
  across seasons.
\item
  \textbf{s(living\_area), edf = 9.0:} The most non-linear term in the
  model. The consumption-area relationship is not monotone, meaning
  medium-sized homes may behave very differently from small or very
  large ones. This possibly reflects different heating system
  configurations or occupancy patterns.
\end{itemize}

The \textbf{R² of 30.3\%} tells the same story from a different angle.

forecast without household-level data.

kWh is directly actionable, and R² communicates model quality in plain
percentage terms.

\subsection{Neural Network (NN)}\label{nn}

\emph{Lead: Raphaël Spagolla}

\subsubsection{Objective}\label{objective-4}

The aim of this analysis is to predict whether an observation
corresponds to a high-consumption event (high\_consumption), a binary
outcome taking values 0 or 1. This is a classification problem, where
the focus is on accurately distinguishing between high- and
low-consumption cases.

From a practical perspective, reliable classification of
high-consumption events can support decision-making processes such as
resource allocation, cost management, or demand planning. Early
identification of such events allows for more efficient operational
responses.

A neural network is employed to capture potentially complex and
non-linear relationships between consumption and predictors, including
weather conditions, building characteristics, and household composition.
Model performance is evaluated based on predictive accuracy and
classification metrics derived from test data.

\subsubsection{Data Preparation}\label{data-preparation}

The dataset consists of 99,838 observations and includes variables
related to weather conditions, building characteristics, and household
composition. The response variable, \emph{high\_consumption}, is binary
and approximately balanced between the two classes.

To ensure valid model evaluation, the data is split into training (80\%)
and test (20\%) sets using a stratified sampling approach. This
preserves the class distribution in both subsets and avoids biased
performance estimates that could arise from imbalanced splits.

Categorical variables are transformed into dummy variables using one-hot
encoding, allowing them to be used as inputs in the neural network. In
addition, all predictors are scaled based on the training data. This
step is important for neural networks, as it improves numerical
stability and facilitates efficient training. The same transformations
are then applied to the test set to ensure consistency and prevent data
leakage.

\subsection{Support Vector Machine (SVM)}\label{svm}

\emph{Lead: Tharrmeehan Krishnathasan}

\subsubsection{Objective}\label{objective-5}

The aim of this analysis is to classify each day as a
\emph{high-consumption day} (top 25\% of daily kWh usage) or a
\emph{normal day} --- a binary classification problem. This framing is
practically useful: identifying high-load days in advance allows grid
operators and energy providers to plan capacity and issue consumption
alerts.

A Support Vector Machine (SVM) is employed because it can capture
complex, non- linear decision boundaries via kernel functions, without
requiring distributional assumptions on the predictors. Model
performance is evaluated on a held-out test set using accuracy,
sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score.

\subsubsection{Data Preparation}\label{data-preparation-1}

The response variable, \texttt{high\_consumption}, is derived from
\texttt{log\_kWh\_total} using the 75th percentile as a threshold. Days
above this threshold are labelled \emph{high}; all others are
\emph{normal}. The threshold corresponds to 3.541 on the log scale
(approximately 34.5 kWh per day). This data-driven cutoff identifies the
25\% of days with the highest energy demand, which are
disproportionately responsible for grid stress events.

After removing missing values, the dataset contains 93,686 observations:
23,440 high-consumption days (25.0\%) and 70,246 normal days (75.0\%).
The original \texttt{log\_kWh\_total} is excluded from the predictors to
prevent data leakage --- the SVM must identify high-load days from
weather conditions and household characteristics alone.

The data are split into training (74,949 rows) and test (18,737 rows)
sets using stratified 80/20 sampling, preserving the 25/75 class ratio
in both subsets. All numeric predictors are standardised before
training, as SVMs are sensitive to feature scale.

\subsubsection{Model Setup}\label{model-setup-5}

Two SVM models are estimated using a radial basis function (RBF) kernel.
The RBF kernel maps data into a high-dimensional space, enabling
non-linear decision boundaries --- well-suited to energy consumption
patterns that interact across weather and household variables.

\textbf{Baseline SVM:} Cost = 1, gamma = default (1 / number of
features). This serves as a reference point for the tuned model.

\textbf{Tuned SVM:} 5-fold cross-validation is used to search over a
grid of \texttt{C} ∈ \{1, 10, 100\} and \texttt{sigma} ∈ \{0.01, 0.1,
1/7\}. \texttt{C} controls the penalty for misclassification (higher =
tighter fit); \texttt{sigma} is the inverse bandwidth of the RBF kernel
(higher = more local boundaries). The grid search and cross-validation
are performed internally by \texttt{e1071::tune()}, which evaluates
every parameter combination and returns the model with the lowest
cross-validation error. The best parameter combination maximises
cross-validation accuracy on the training set.

The tuned model uses the following best parameters: C = 100, sigma =
0.1429 (equal to 1/7, the default gamma in \texttt{e1071}). These were
selected from a 3×3 grid and achieved the highest cross-validation
accuracy of 0.8304 (Kappa = 0.4859).

The more important metrics for this task are:

\textbf{Sensitivity: 0.4834} --- the model correctly identifies only
48.3\% of actual high-consumption days. More than half of all high-load
days are missed. This is a significant limitation for an early-warning
application where false negatives carry higher operational costs.

\textbf{Specificity: 0.9481} --- normal days are classified correctly
94.8\% of the time. The model is conservative: it flags fewer
high-consumption days than actually occur (2995 predicted high vs.~4688
actual high in the test set).

\textbf{F1 Score: 0.5899} --- the harmonic mean of precision and recall
for the high class reflects the imbalance between high specificity and
low sensitivity.

\textbf{Kappa: 0.4905} --- a moderate level of agreement beyond chance,
improving on the baseline (0.4602) but still below the 0.6 threshold
typically considered \emph{substantial} agreement.

The tuned model is selected as the final model based on the highest

\subsubsection{Model Diagnostics}\label{model-diagnostics-5}

SVM diagnostics focus on generalisation performance and the validity of
modelling choices rather than distributional assumptions.

\textbf{CV tuning results:} Across all 9 combinations, CV accuracy
ranged from 0.8071 (C=10, sigma=0.01) to 0.8304 (C=100, sigma=0.1429).
The top four combinations all involve sigma ≥ 0.1, suggesting the model
benefits from a moderately local kernel. The gain from increasing C from
10 to 100 at sigma=0.1429 is small (0.8268 → 0.8304), indicating
diminishing returns from further penalising misclassification.

\textbf{Class separability:} Sensitivity of 48.3\% reveals that the
available predictors (weather conditions and household characteristics)
do not reliably separate the two classes. Many high-consumption days
fall in the same feature space as normal days, creating an irreducible
overlap that no kernel or parameter choice can overcome.

\textbf{Tuning stability:} The best parameter combination (C=100,
sigma=0.1429) sits at the top-right corner of the grid, raising the
question of whether a larger C or sigma would further improve results.
However, given the flattening improvement from C=10 to C=100 and the
domain knowledge that the classes genuinely overlap, additional tuning
is unlikely to produce meaningful sensitivity gains.

\textbf{Comparison with baseline:} The tuned model improves accuracy
(82.2\% → 83.2\%) and sensitivity (46.2\% → 48.3\%) over the default
parameterisation. The improvement is real but modest, consistent with
the view that the bottleneck is feature informativeness rather than
model configuration.

\subsubsection{Key Findings}\label{key-findings-4}

\textbf{The SVM achieves reasonable accuracy but low sensitivity:} At
83.2\% accuracy, the model clearly outperforms always predicting the
majority class (75\%). However, sensitivity of 48.3\% means it misses
more than half of all actual high- consumption days --- the most
operationally important class.

\textbf{High specificity reflects a conservative classifier:}
Specificity of 94.8\% shows the model rarely raises false alarms on
normal days, but this comes at the cost of missing many genuine
high-load events.

\textbf{Cross-validation tuning yields real but limited improvement:}
Moving from default parameters to C=100, sigma=0.1429 improves
sensitivity from 46.2\% to 48.3\% and accuracy from 82.2\% to 83.2\%.
The gain is consistent but small, confirming that further tuning alone
cannot resolve the core problem.

\textbf{The predictor set is the bottleneck:} Weather conditions and
static household characteristics (building type, living area, residents,
heat pump type) capture only part of what drives high-consumption days.
The strong day-to-day within- household variation that drives a day over
the 34.5 kWh threshold is not fully explained by the available features.

\textbf{Feature leakage is correctly avoided:} By excluding
\texttt{log\_kWh\_total}, all results reflect genuine out-of-sample
discriminative ability from operationally observable inputs.

\subsubsection{Conclusion}\label{conclusion-5}

The SVM demonstrates that weather conditions and household
characteristics carry real signal for identifying high-consumption days,
achieving \textasciitilde8 percentage points above the naive
majority-class baseline. The model is well-specified and the tuning is
sound.

However, the model is of \textbf{limited practical usefulness as a
standalone early- warning system}. A sensitivity of 48.3\% means that in
a deployment context, more than half of high-load days would go
undetected. For capacity planning or demand-response triggering, this
miss rate is likely too high to be operationally reliable.

The root cause is not the model choice --- it is the available feature
set. High and normal consumption days overlap substantially in the
weather-and-household feature space, and no kernel function or
regularisation choice can separate what is genuinely overlapping in the
data. Meaningful improvement would require richer features: lagged
consumption, day-of-week and seasonal indicators, occupancy patterns, or
appliance-level data.

If the threshold for flagging is lowered (accepting more false alarms to
capture more true positives), the model could play a supporting role in
probabilistic forecasting pipelines. But at the default threshold, it
should not be used as the sole basis for high-consumption day decisions.

\section{Use of Generative AI}\label{ai}

Generative AI tools (primarily Claude and GitHub Copilot) were used
throughout this project for code generation, debugging, and text
drafting. Concretely, AI assisted with writing R code for data
preprocessing pipelines, model fitting wrappers, cross-validation loops,
and visualisation. It also helped explain statistical concepts such as
overdispersion in Poisson models, the interpretation of GAM smooth
terms, and the role of the cost and kernel bandwidth parameters in SVMs.
Straightforward, well-scoped tasks were handled reliably: generating
boilerplate ggplot2 code, converting between data formats, and producing
first drafts of prose that described model outputs. More demanding tasks
required significant human oversight: AI-generated cross-validation code
contained subtle bugs (for example, returning single-column data frames
instead of vectors, and using a mismatched response variable across
models), and AI-drafted coefficient interpretations occasionally
overstated certainty or omitted important caveats. Certain tasks were
not delegable at all --- the substantive analytical decisions, such as
choosing which threshold to use for the high-consumption binary response
and how to handle the incompatibility between the Neural Network's
pre-computed predictions and the rest of the CV pipeline, required
domain judgement that the AI could frame but not resolve. All
AI-generated code was reviewed, tested, and where necessary corrected
before being incorporated, and all interpretive prose was verified
against the actual model output rather than accepted at face value.
